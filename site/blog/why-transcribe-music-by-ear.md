Transcribing music by ear means listening to a recording and working out what's being played, then playing it back or writing it down. At first it's slow and often frustrating. It's also the best way I know to improve your ear. The biggest benefit: a recording teaches you things that sheet music and tabs leave out.

## Your ear, on real music

Interval drills and chord-quality recognition have their place. Practising one skill on its own is the fastest way to get better at it. Think of how children learn addition. They could learn it by adding up prices at the grocery store, but they'd get a few sums a week, mixed in with everything else going on. So instead they practise the sums themselves, 2 + 4 = 6, over and over, until adding is automatic. Interval and chord drills do the same for your ear.

But in the end you have to add up a real bill. Drills use clean, isolated sounds, and songs don't. Notes get bent, a chord is spread across three instruments, and the bass sits under the kick drum. Transcribing trains you to hear through that, which is what you need when you're actually playing with other people.

Learning from a real recording can also train parts of your hearing you didn't know existed. Take the guitar solo in _Whole Lotta Love_. It isn't especially fast or technical, but it's full of notes that don't really exist on the piano, bent somewhere between the keys, and working out what each one is takes a different kind of listening.

You could build a drill for notes like these, but it would be hard, and it couldn't cover every bend and slide a real player uses. Drills are still a good way to practise one thing at a time. But we don't learn addition to get good at addition problems; we learn it to use it in everyday life. Ear training is the same: the point is to use your ear in the real world, on real music, not only on drills.

## Feel, which notation can't hold

Two players can read the same eight bars and sound nothing alike. The difference is in how far behind the beat a note lands, how long it's held, which notes are ghosted and which are leaned on. None of that is on the page.

When you transcribe, you copy those details along with the notes. You notice the guitarist laying back on the upbeats, or the singer sliding into the last note of each line, and when you play it back that way, some of it stays in your own playing.

## Vocabulary

A lick you've worked out yourself, phrase by phrase, sticks in a way a lick read off a page doesn't. After a few songs you start hearing the same ideas turn up elsewhere, and eventually in your own improvising. Jazz players have always learned this way, from records, but it works for any style.

It also makes theory easier. Once you've found that a chorus moves to the relative minor, the term is just a name for something you've already heard.

## Why not look up the tab?

Tabs are useful, and there's no shame in using them. But they give you the answer and skip the part where you learn something. They're also often wrong, and they rarely show timing or feel. The recording is the source, and once you can work from it directly you can learn any song, not just the ones somebody has written out.

## Can't an app do it for me?

There are apps that listen to a recording and write out the notes. If all you need is a chart for tonight's gig, use one. But the notes on paper were never the point. What matters is what happens to your ear while you work them out yourself, and an app can't do that part for you, the same way a calculator doesn't teach you to add.

## How to start

1. **Pick something short and clear,** such as a bass line, a vocal melody or a simple riff, in a song you already know well. For chords, here are [eight easy songs to start with](/blog/easy-songs-to-transcribe/).
2. **Listen until you can sing it** along with the recording, before you pick up your instrument.
3. **Loop one phrase,** a few bars at most, and stay on it until you have it.
4. **Find the first note.** The rest of the phrase is much easier once you have it.
5. **Slow it down when notes blur together.** At 70 % or even 50 % you can hear each note, and as long as the pitch stays put, you can play along.
6. **Play along at full speed** to check. If something sounds off, go back to that phrase.

Writing it down is optional. It helps you remember and study a part, but plenty of players never write a note.

The first song takes a long time. The next ones go faster.
