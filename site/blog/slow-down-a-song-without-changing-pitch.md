To slow down a song without changing the pitch, play it in a player that time-stretches: it changes the speed and leaves the pitch alone. YouTube, VLC and Audacity can all do it. To learn a song, a player made for transcribing is quicker, because it loops the passage you're working on while you change the speed: [EarBee](/) is a free, open-source one (I make it, so weigh my opinion accordingly). For practice, start at about 70 %, go down to 50 % when the notes blur together, and work back up to full speed.

Below: why slowing down usually changes the pitch, what time-stretching does instead, how slow to go, and why very slow speeds sound strange.

## Why slowing down usually changes the pitch

Play a record at the wrong speed, or slow down a tape, and the song drops in pitch as well as in tempo. A note's pitch is how fast it vibrates. Play the recording slower and every vibration in it slows down too, so the whole song sinks. At half speed it's a full octave lower, and the singer sounds like a sleepy giant. Speed it up and you get chipmunks.

For a long time that was the only way. Players who learned from records slowed them down anyway and worked out the notes an octave low. It worked, but the speed and the pitch came as a pair.

## What time-stretching does instead

Time-stretching pulls the two apart. The player cuts the sound into very short, overlapping slices, a few hundredths of a second each, spaces them further apart and blends them back together. Inside each slice the vibrations keep their speed, so the pitch stays where it was. There are just more slices to get through, so the music takes longer.

That's the idea, at least. Real time-stretchers are cleverer about where and how the slices join, which is why a good one sounds natural at moderate speeds and a poor one sounds wobbly. EarBee uses a very good one, [Signalsmith Stretch](https://signalsmith-audio.co.uk/code/stretch/). Done well, a song at 70 % sounds like the same band playing more slowly.

## How to slow down a song to learn it

The method is the same whatever player you use:

1. **Pick a short passage and loop it:** one phrase, a few bars at most. You'll hear it many times, and it's easier when it starts again by itself.
2. **Slow it to about 70 %.** If the notes still run together, go down to 50 %.
3. **Listen until you can sing it,** then find it on your instrument and play along with the slowed-down recording.
4. **Speed it up a little at a time,** a few percent at each step, as long as you can still play it clean.
5. **Check at full speed.** Play along with the original. If something sounds off, go back to that phrase and slow it down again.

## How slow should you go?

As slow as you need, and no slower. Roughly:

- **80–90 %:** for most rhythm parts and melodies, when you only need a little more time to follow.
- **70 %:** a good place to start for anything you can't follow at full speed.
- **50 %:** fast runs, ornaments, quick strumming: anything where the notes blur together.
- **Below 50 %:** for a moment, to hear one note or one change you can't catch otherwise.

Why not always go very slow? Because at a quarter of its speed, a phrase stops sounding like a phrase. The notes are all there, but the shape that makes them music, where the line is heading and which note is leaned on, is hard to hear once it's stretched that far. And feel lives in the timing: [how far behind the beat a note lands](/blog/why-transcribe-music-by-ear/) is hard to feel when everything is stretched four times over.

So slow down to find the notes, and speed back up to learn how they're played.

## Why it sounds strange at very slow speeds

The slower you go, the more even a good time-stretcher shows its seams. What you may hear:

- **Smeared attacks.** Drums, plucked strings and piano notes lose their sharp start, or seem to hit twice.
- **A watery, phasey sound,** as if the music were coming through water or a spinning fan.
- **Longer echoes.** The reverb is part of the recording, so it's stretched too, and fills the gaps.

That's normal: the slicing that keeps the pitch also blurs what happens between the slices. It isn't your file, and your player isn't broken. At slow speeds, trust the pitch, since a held note still sounds at the right pitch, but judge rhythm, attack and tone at full speed or close to it.

## The other way round: same speed, different pitch

The same technique works the other way: a player can shift the pitch and keep the speed. That helps when a recording doesn't match your instrument. Bands often tune down a half step or more, and some old recordings sound slightly sharp or flat because the tape ran at a slightly different speed. Shift the recording in semitones for a band that tuned down, and in cents for a small difference, and you can play along without retuning. Shifting a bass line up an octave helps too: it then sounds in your guitar's range, and its notes are much easier to pick out. ## Free ways to slow down a song

You may not need to install anything:

- **YouTube:** the player's speed setting keeps the pitch, down to a quarter of the speed. There's no built-in way to loop just one passage, though.
- **VLC:** keeps the pitch when you change the speed, and has an A→B loop (turn on **View › Advanced controls**). There's no waveform, so you set the loop by clicking at the right moment while it plays.
- **Audacity:** a clip's Pitch and Speed setting slows it down and keeps the pitch, but you set it in a dialog, not while it plays.

They're fine for an occasional riff. For a whole solo, you'll want a player that does it all in one place, such as EarBee: a loop you can draw on a waveform, a speed you can change while it plays, and a quick way back to the start of the phrase.
