Is ear training worth it? I'd say yes, but it depends on what you mean by ear training. Pressing the name of an interval in an app is a small slice of what used to be considered the basic skills of every musician. Singing, tapping, reading and writing music, and then hearing all of it in real songs, is one of the most useful things you can practise.

This post is the other side of [why transcribing music by ear makes you a better musician](/blog/why-transcribe-music-by-ear/): that one is about learning from recordings, this one is about the drills.

## What a drill does well

A drill takes one skill out of the music and gives you a lot of it. In ten minutes, an app can play you fifty clean intervals and tell you straight away whether you got each one right. It's the same reason children practise sums on their own before they add up a real bill: repetition and quick feedback, on one thing at a time.

What a typical app doesn't do is ask anything of you except to listen and press a button. Recognising something is the easiest part of knowing it. Can you sing the interval you just named, or write down the rhythm you just heard? That's the part that turns listening into musicianship.

## Learn it like reading, not like a test

All of this needs some educational common sense. When you first learn to read, nobody shows you the letters ("this is a, this is b, this is c…") and then says "now just read!" You start by repeating syllables after somebody, and only slowly get to reading on your own.

Ear training works the same way. When somebody plays you an interval and you sing it back, that's like repeating a word after someone before you can read it yourself. It isn't cheating; it's how learning starts.

And real life isn't a test. Nobody is marking your answers, so use all the help you can get: play the interval back, sing it, sing the high note, then the low note, play the bottom note, and sing it again. Just keep challenging yourself, so that you need a little less help each time. That's how a child goes from repeating syllables to reading a whole book.

It's peculiar how common sense goes out the window once music is involved. Apparently people don't learn and get better; they're just talented. They don't learn to read music; they can just do it. If there's one thing I'd like you to take away from this post, it's this: anything, musical or not, can be learned, and has to be learned, and the more time, attention and proper method you put into it, the better you'll get.

So always question the orthodoxy. To break some of yours, read [_Absolute pitch can be learned by some adults_](https://apexlab.uchicago.edu/docs/papers/AbsolutePitchLearned.pdf), a study from the University of Chicago in which adults were trained in absolute pitch, an ability usually thought to be out of reach unless you start in early childhood.

## Basic ear training exercises

Most drills have a passive form (listen and name it) and an active one (sing it, tap it or write it down). I'd always go for the active one:

- **Intervals:** don't just name them; sing one from a given note, then check it on your instrument.
- **Chord quality:** don't just say major or minor; sing the chord's notes from the bottom up.
- **Rhythm:** don't just clap it back; write it down, or tap the beat while you sing it.
- **Singing back:** sing a short melody before you play it. Simple, but it underpins all the others.
- **Sight-singing:** sing a short melody from the page, without playing it first.
- **Dictation:** write down a short melody or rhythm you hear.

### Before intervals: the basics

One part of ear training that I think often gets left out is what I'd call "basic musical aptitude". For a beginner, even something as simple as intervals (simple for a seasoned musician, at least) can be too much at first. So it helps to practise more basic skills first: telling a high note from a low one, singing a single note in tune, and noticing when the chord changes. Not what kind of chord it is, or what kind of cadence, just that a change happened. I may write about a more direct way to practise these in a future post.

Fittingly, the book I'm about to mention starts in exactly this place: its first chapter is about high, medium and low tones.

## Basic musicianship: reading, sight-singing and dictation

Another thing that's almost ignored when people talk casually about a musician's skills is what used to be considered basic musicianship. Here I'll point to Paul Hindemith's book [_Elementary Training for Musicians_](https://books.google.com/books?id=xbu7AAAAIAAJ). Hindemith was appalled at the state of musical education in his day (his day being 1946), and the book is his answer: a compilation of methods that already existed in other works, but had never been put together into a full programme. The whole book is a step-by-step collection of exercises that takes you from zero to having these basic musicianship skills.

Its central idea is activity. His advice to teachers: "Never teach anything without demonstrating it by writing and singing, or playing; check each exercise by a counter-exercise that uses other means of expression." The point is to turn the student "from an attentive listener into a working musician".

I won't go into much depth here, but being able to recognise intervals isn't enough. Reading music, sight-singing and musical dictation (writing down what you hear) are crucial skills too.

"But I don't want to learn to read music," or "I can't read music." Some people will tell you that you don't have to, and that's fine. What I would say is that you don't need to be able to read in order to speak, either, but think about what language makes possible once writing is involved.

## Is interval ear training worth it?

Yes, if you do it with your voice and a pencil as well as your ears. Intervals are how you get from one note of a melody to the next, but naming them in an app is the weakest way to know them. What you want is to sing any interval from any note, write it down when you hear it, and spot it inside a real melody, where it goes by much faster than in a drill.

## How much ear training per day?

Little and often: a short session every day does more than a long one every week. Stop when you start guessing, because from then on the drill is mostly training your guessing.

## How long does ear training take?

For the whole of the basics, Hindemith reckoned his book would keep a class and its teacher busy for a year and a half or two years. After that it never really ends, but each skill you get down shows up in your playing straight away.

## Why ear training feels so hard

- **Too much at once.** All twelve intervals at once is hard for anyone. Start with two that sound very different, and add more as they get easy.
- **The drill trap.** Your score goes up, but songs don't get any easier, because you've got good at the app, not at music. Hindemith complained of exercise books so dry "that even the most docile user cannot see their relationship to living music". Use each skill on real songs.
- **Talent stops being enough.** Hindemith noticed that even talented students reach a point where instinct alone doesn't carry them, and only steady practice does. That's normal, not a sign that you're unmusical.

## Drills and real music, together

In my view, the best way is to do both at once:

1. **Work through the basics in order,** in a book like Hindemith's, without skipping the parts that feel too simple.
2. **Transcribe real music alongside,** something short and clear that you'd like to play; here are [eight easy songs to start with](/blog/easy-songs-to-transcribe/). Working out a recording is dictation, with music you've never seen written down.
3. **Write down at least some of what you transcribe,** for practice in dictation and reading at once.
4. **When a song shows you a gap,** go back to that part of the course and work on it.

## So, is it worth it?

Yes, as long as it's the whole thing: hearing music clearly, singing it, reading it and writing it down, well enough to play what you hear. A high score in an app was never the aim.
