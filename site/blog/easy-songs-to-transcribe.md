Easy songs to transcribe have chords that repeat, change slowly and sit clearly in the mix. These eight go roughly from easiest to hardest: the first ones repeat a short loop, and the later ones bring in a moving bass and changes you don't see coming.

There are no chord charts here: working them out is the point. Each song comes with a way in instead. The tips are for guitar, but the songs work on any instrument.

If you're wondering whether it's worth the effort, start with [why transcribing music by ear makes you a better musician](/blog/why-transcribe-music-by-ear/).

## 1. _Knockin' on Heaven's Door_ by Bob Dylan

_Beginner · a repeating progression_

The progression comes round again and again in the same order, so you can hear each change coming and check it every time. The chords are played clearly and the guitar is in standard tuning, so you can find them on your own guitar. Loop a single chord in the recording, so that only that chord plays, and try open chords over it until one sounds nearly the same.

## 2. _Three Little Birds_ by Bob Marley & The Wailers

_Beginner · the home chord_

Start by finding the home chord, the one the song keeps returning to. Then work out where the others lead away from it.

## 3. _Let It Be_ by The Beatles

_Beginner · strong chord changes_

A piano song. The changes are clear and the melody is easy to sing, which helps you keep your place. It also shows how much the instrument and the voicing matter: a chord can sound quite different on the piano, especially with a different note at the bottom (an inversion). Struggle with it for a while, write down what you have, and then compare it with another source, such as a published transcription.

## 4. _Stand by Me_ by Ben E. King

_Beginner · the bass line_

The bass line is the way into this song: it plays the root of each chord, and the pattern repeats, so you can check every change you think you've heard. The bass sounds much lower than your guitar, which makes its notes hard to pick out. Shift the recording up 12 semitones (an octave): the bass then sounds like single notes on your guitar, and it's much easier to follow.

## 5. _House of the Rising Sun_ by The Animals

_Beginner to intermediate · arpeggios_

The guitar picks out each chord as an arpeggio, and its lowest note is accented every time. Slow the song down and listen for that note: it's the one the chord is named after. Then find out whether the chord is major or minor by playing both over the recording. The wrong one is easy to spot, because a major chord played over a minor one clashes badly. As for whether the guitar is in standard tuning, I'll let you work that out (hint, hint).

## 6. _Can't Help Falling in Love_ by Elvis Presley

_Beginner to intermediate · bass movement_

Slow enough to hear every change, with a bass that moves under the chords. The chords are arpeggiated, so you may be able to work out their notes before you know what the chords are called. Looking up a chord's name from its notes isn't cheating: you're learning just enough theory to keep going. Say you hear C, E and G and don't know what chord that is. Look it up, and you've learned that it's C major.

## 7. _Have You Ever Seen the Rain_ by Creedence Clearwater Revival

_Beginner to intermediate · verse and chorus_

A clear verse and chorus, so you can work out one section at a time. The guitar strums fast, so use the method from the first song: loop one chord and try open chords over it until one matches.

## 8. _Yesterday_ by The Beatles

_Intermediate · beyond simple loops_

More changes, a moving bass, and chords that don't always land where you expect, so save it for when the others feel easy. The guitar is tuned a whole step down: shift the recording up two semitones and it matches a guitar in standard tuning. Then be patient. There are a lot of chords, but most of them are ordinary open and barre chords.

## If it's hard at first

Transcribing is a hard skill, and even the basics take time to learn. If the first song on this list beats you, that's normal, and it doesn't mean you lack an ear for it. Keep at it: work out what you can, check it, and come back to the parts you couldn't get. That struggle is ear training at its most direct, and it gets smaller. Each song goes a little faster than the last, and one day you'll hear a change on the first listen that used to take you an evening.
