Looking for a free alternative to Transcribe!? There are a few good ones, and which is best depends on what you transcribe. If you want one app made for the job, with a waveform, loops, marks and video, try [EarBee](/) (I make it, so weigh my opinion accordingly). If you'd rather use something you may already have, VLC slows music down without changing the pitch, and Sonic Visualiser is a free program for close listening, with a steeper learning curve.

Below: what Transcribe! does and costs, what a transcribing tool actually needs, and five free options, with what each one is good at and what it leaves out.

## What Transcribe! is, and what it costs

[Transcribe!](https://seventhstring.com/xscribe/overview.html), from Seventh String Software, has been the standard tool for working out music by ear for a long time, and for good reason. It slows a recording down to a twentieth of its speed while keeping the pitch, shifts the pitch by cents or by up to three octaves, keeps named loops and markers, has an EQ, shows video, and has a spectrum view that suggests which notes are sounding and even guesses chords.

It runs on Windows, macOS and Linux. After a 30-day trial, a licence is [$39](https://seventhstring.com/xscribe/buy.html), paid once, with lower prices per copy when several are bought together. That's fair for what it does, and if you transcribe every day, it may well be money well spent. But if you're starting out, or you only transcribe now and then, it's worth knowing what you can do for free.

## What you need for transcribing

Before comparing tools, it helps to know what actually matters when you work something out by ear. In rough order of importance:

1. **Slowing down without changing the pitch.** At half speed, a fast run becomes a string of notes you can follow, but only if it's still in the same key. Slowing a tape changes both; time-stretching keeps the pitch.
2. **Looping a short passage.** You'll hear the same two seconds dozens of times. The loop should be quick to set, ideally by dragging on a waveform, and quick to move on.
3. **Getting around from the keyboard.** Replaying the last phrase, stepping back a little, starting the loop again: if each one needs the mouse, your hands keep leaving your instrument.
4. **Marking the song.** Verse, chorus, solo, "bar 12 is the hard bit". Marks let you map the structure first and then work section by section.
5. **EQ and pitch shift.** Turning up the low end to hear the bass, cutting it to hear the guitar, or moving a recording that's a half step flat into standard tuning.
6. **Video,** when you're learning from a lesson or a live performance and want to see the hands.

A spectrum view or note guessing is nice to have, but it isn't essential: it's a hint, and your ear still has to decide. If you're new to this, [why transcribing by ear makes you a better musician](/blog/why-transcribe-music-by-ear/) explains why doing the work yourself is the point.

## Five free alternatives

### 1. EarBee

[EarBee](/) is a free, open-source player (MIT licence) for Windows, macOS and Linux, made for one purpose: transcribing music by ear. Not a media player with a speed slider added, but a tool built around that work, whether you're writing a song out as sheet music or tab, or working it out to get better at hearing and playing. It covers the whole list above. It slows a song or a video without changing the pitch, shifts the pitch in semitones and cents, and has an EQ. You draw a loop on the waveform, mark points and named sections on the track, and step through a phrase from the keyboard, with one key that replays from where you started listening. It can also find the beats and bars, and then loop the bar you're in and move the loop on phrase by phrase. Videos stay in sync at any speed, and you can paste a YouTube link to open one. A project file keeps your marks, loop, speed and EQ.

What it doesn't have: a spectrum view or note and chord guessing. There's [no trial, no paid version and no account](/#free).

### 2. Sonic Visualiser

[Sonic Visualiser](https://sonicvisualiser.org/), from Queen Mary University of London, is free software (GPL) for Windows, macOS and Linux, built for studying recordings closely. It slows playback down to an eighth of the speed with the Rubber Band time-stretcher, so the pitch stays put, loops a selection, and lets you add labelled points and annotation layers. Its spectrograms are excellent, and plugins can add analysis such as beat tracking or note estimation.

The catch is that it's a research tool first: powerful, but with a lot of panes, layers and settings to learn before it feels quick. It plays audio only, not video.

### 3. VLC

You may already have [VLC](https://www.videolan.org/vlc/). It keeps the pitch when you change the playback speed (its audio time-stretching is on by default), plays almost any audio or video file, and has an A→B loop: turn on **View › Advanced controls**, then click the button once at the start of the passage and once at the end.

It's a general player, though. There's no waveform, so setting a loop precisely means clicking at the right moment while it plays, and there are no section marks or quick ways to nudge a loop along. It does have a graphic equaliser (on Windows and Linux, under **Tools › Effects and Filters**). Fine for an occasional riff; tiring for a whole solo.

### 4. Audacity

[Audacity](https://www.audacityteam.org/) is a free audio editor with a waveform, labels and loop playback. Its Play-at-Speed control [changes the pitch](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/play_at_speed_toolbar.html) along with the speed, but a clip's own Pitch and Speed setting slows it down and keeps the pitch, without changing the audio itself. The catch is that you set the speed in a dialog, and [not while it plays](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/audacity_tracks_and_clips.html): to try another speed, you stop, change it, and start again. It works, but it's an editor, and it feels like one. It's great for cutting a passage out to practise with.

### 5. Play it Slowly

[Play it Slowly](https://29a.ch/playitslowly/) is a small, simple Linux app: open a file, change the speed or pitch, loop a part. It does that well, but it hasn't had a release in about ten years, and it's Linux only.

### And YouTube itself

If the song is on YouTube, the player's own speed setting goes down to 0.25× and keeps the pitch. There's no built-in way to loop just one passage, so it's best for a quick listen at a slower speed.

## When Transcribe! is still worth paying for

To be fair to it, Transcribe! does a few things none of the free options above do all together:

- **Big pitch shifts.** It can move a recording by up to three octaves, which helps with a low bass line or a very high part: shift it to where your ear catches it more easily.
- **Note and chord hints** on a piano keyboard, right where you're working. Sonic Visualiser can get close with plugins, but less directly.
- **Decades of refinement,** foot-pedal support, and a large set of configurable shortcuts.

If those matter to you, try its 30-day trial alongside a free tool, on the same song, and see which one you reach for.

## Which one should you choose?

- **You want one tool made for transcribing:** EarBee, or Transcribe! if you'll pay for the extras above.
- **You want to see the music in detail** (spectrograms, analysis plugins), and don't mind learning: Sonic Visualiser.
- **You only work out a riff now and then:** VLC is probably already on your computer.
- **You need to edit the audio too:** Audacity, alongside one of the others.

Whichever you pick, the tool matters less than the habit: a short passage, slowed down, looped until you can sing it, then played. If you need something to practise on, here are [eight easy songs to transcribe](/blog/easy-songs-to-transcribe/).

## Why AI transcription won't replace transcribing by ear

There are now apps that listen to a recording and hand you the chords, or even the notes. They're getting better, and they'll keep getting better. So why bother with any of the tools above?

Because for most musicians, the transcription was never the point. You don't work out a solo by ear because the world needs another copy of it written down; you do it because of what it does to you. Hearing a phrase, singing it, finding it on your instrument and getting it under your fingers is how your ear gets sharper, how your playing gets more fluent, and how a style ends up in your hands. The page of notes at the end is a by-product.

An AI can give you the by-product. It can't do the learning for you, any more than a forklift can lift weights for you at the gym. However accurate these apps become, that won't change, because the reason to transcribe isn't a gap in the technology; it's the work itself.

That doesn't make them useless. Once you've worked something out yourself, an automatic chord chart can be a quick way to check your answer, as long as you remember it can be wrong too. But the listening, the singing and the finding are yours to do, and that's what a transcribing tool is for: making that work easier, not doing it in your place. Drills have their place too: [is ear training worth it?](/blog/is-ear-training-worth-it/) is about what they can and can't do.
